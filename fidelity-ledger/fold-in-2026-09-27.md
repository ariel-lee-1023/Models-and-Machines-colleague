## Worked design application: revisiting a persistent interface

This is a new synthesis, not an empirical case or model-run output. Suppose a lab proposes replacing an established instrumentation interface. First distinguish the candidate's working principle and compatible components from evidence that it functions across the lab's actual timing, environmental and recovery conditions. A successful prototype is evidence of embodiment, not evidence of dependable deployment at every bench. Identify whether adoption requires new adapters, calibration practices, operator training or supplier support.

Then reconstruct the incumbent loop: more installed instruments can attract more compatible tools and trained staff, increasing its attractiveness to later purchasers. Treat that as a hypothesis to check against procurement and maintenance histories. Alternatives may have failed technically, remained immature, or lost complements; do not collapse these into a single selection story. Compare current system performance with the costed migration, and keep any equal-development counterfactual separate.

A conditional intervention could preserve a limited alternative deployment, test an adapter and train a small group while retaining a fallback. An engineering owner would compare integration faults, maintenance workload, performance and operator competence across matched tasks, allowing enough time for training and failures to become visible. Stop or revise if interoperability adds hidden errors or support costs overwhelm the benefit. If the interface alone was the constraint, further basic invention may be unnecessary; if the new phenomenon cannot be controlled, adoption incentives will not solve that problem. These proposed measures need case-specific design and evidence.
