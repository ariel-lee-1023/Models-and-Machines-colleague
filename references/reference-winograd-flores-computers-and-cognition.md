## Mental Model (read first)

[M1,M3,M4] Winograd and Flores shift design from modeling a detached rational subject toward supporting people already engaged in meaningful activities and commitments. Computers operate in formal domains, but the significance of those operations depends on background understanding. Design can change that background and open new possibilities; it does more than reproduce an existing representation of work.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Chapters 1–3: Background, being, and breakdown

- [M1,M3] **The Question of Design; The Rationalistic Tradition** (chs. 1–2): the rationalistic orientation describes a world of identifiable objects and problems solved through representations and rules. The authors challenge its status as a general account of understanding, while recognizing the usefulness of formal methods in appropriate domains. Do not confuse a critique of that foundation with an injunction to stop calculating.
- [M1,M4] **Hermeneutics; Pre-understanding** (ch. 3): interpretation begins within a background of practices and distinctions. It is not assembled from context-free meanings. When a user and a system apparently share a term, investigate whether they share the practical background that makes it usable.
- [M1,M3] **Thrownness; Readiness-to-hand; Breakdown** (§§3.3–3.4): people must act from within ongoing involvement, without a complete representation or final neutral standpoint. Familiar equipment recedes during fluent use; breakdown makes aspects of it and its use explicit. Breakdown can reveal design possibilities rather than only count as an exception to suppress.

### Chapters 4–6: Cognition, language, and commitments

- [M1,M4] **Autopoiesis; Closure of the Nervous System** (ch. 4): drawing on Maturana, the authors treat cognition through an organism's organization and structural relations rather than a simple transfer of information into an internal picture. Organizational closure does not mean isolation from causal interaction. This is the book's theoretical orientation, not a generic empirical result about all artificial systems.
- [M1,M4] **Consensual Domains; The Observer and Description** (ch. 4): distinctions and descriptions occur within domains of coordinated activity. Identify whose descriptions define the system and what recurrent practice gives them sense. A model's categories are not automatically the categories of its participants.
- [M1,M3] **Language, Listening, and Commitment; Speech Acts** (ch. 5): utterances can request, promise, assert, declare, or otherwise change commitments. Their practical force depends on background and uptake. An information-transfer model misses whether anyone has undertaken an obligation or accepted satisfaction.
- [M1,M3,M4] **Conversation for Action** (§5.4): a request establishes proposed conditions of satisfaction; a response may accept, reject, or counteroffer, and the requester can revise or withdraw. Performance is followed by an assertion of completion and an assessment of satisfaction. The diagram models possibilities in a conversation, not hidden mental states or every possible exchange.
- [M1,M3] **Breakdown and the Ontology of Design** (ch. 6): design brings forth distinctions and practices that reshape a domain. A system can change what counts as work or completion. Examine that transformation along with efficiency inside the current categories.

### Chapters 7–10: Computation and its interpretation

- [M1,M2] **Programming as Representation; Levels of Representation** (ch. 7): a program has formal relations that users interpret as representing something. The interpretation is not supplied solely by symbol manipulation. The M2 connection is an application: attach formal execution properties to a specified abstraction rather than assuming they characterize the whole human activity.
- [M1,M3] **Computation and Intelligence; The Phenomenon of Blindness** (ch. 8): formalization makes some regularities manageable while leaving its constitutive background unavailable within the formal system. When a system fails outside its distinctions, adding more rules need not address the source of blindness.
- [M1,M3] **The Problem of Background; Understanding Language** (ch. 9): successful pattern processing in a circumscribed setting does not establish open-ended understanding. Investigate what users supply and how breakdown is resolved. Claims about the impossibility or promise of an approach belong to the authors' argument, not a present-day capability evaluation.
- [M1,M3] **Expert Systems and Other AI Directions** (ch. 10): assess systems by what their formalized domain permits and excludes. Treat the technologies discussed as historical cases. The durable design distinction is between a useful formal tool and a claim that it has captured the relevant human understanding.

### Chapters 11–12: Organizational design

- [M1,M3,M4] **Organizations as Networks of Commitments** (§11.3): managers coordinate recurrent breakdowns and obligations, not merely optimize choices over a fixed world. Inspect who can make a request, promise action, renegotiate, report completion, and accept it. A workflow can lose the organization's practical structure if it records only data or status.
- [M1,M3] **Resolution and Decision Making; Systematic Domains** (chs. 11–12): applying known rules within an established domain differs from resolving a situation that calls its framing into question. Computers can support recurrent formal work and coordination; design should also leave room for changing the domain and its commitments.

## Worked Example

[M1,M3,M4] **Reconstructed from §5.4: a request and its completion.** A asks B to satisfy a condition. B may promise, decline, or negotiate a different condition; A may change or withdraw the request. After acting, B asserts completion. A's declaration of satisfaction closes the normal sequence; disagreement reopens it. A task tracker that treats B's “done” as equivalent to A's acceptance collapses two different moves. The diagram clarifies this distinction without claiming to represent all motives or contextual negotiations. Applying it to a tracker is a contemporary design inference.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- [M1,M3] If a specification presupposes a shared term, inspect the background practice that gives it meaning.
- [M3,M4] If coordination fails, check the missing commitment or acceptance move before adding notifications.
- [M1,M3] If a breakdown reveals an unanticipated concern, reconsider the domain's distinctions instead of merely patching an exception.
- [M1,M3] When formalizing conversations, preserve ways to contest categories and renegotiate satisfaction; this safeguard follows from reading the framework alongside Suchman.

## Key Takeaways

1. [M1] Formal representations operate within a background they do not exhaust.
2. [M3,M4] Coordination includes commitments and acknowledgment, not just messages.
3. [M3] Design reshapes the domain in which people act.
