# The Coevolution: The Entwined Futures of Humans and Machines — Edward Ashford Lee
**Format**: md | **Sections**: 14 | **Depth**: study | **Type**: text
**Edition**: MIT Press, 2020. Locators use chapter numbers and named subsections.

## Mental Model (read first)

[M1,M3,M4] Lee studies people, technology, and culture as a changing interdependent system. Deliberate design matters, but no designer controls the whole trajectory. The useful engineering question is how to understand and influence present interactions. Evolutionary analogies illuminate selection and dependence; they do not settle biological identity or provide a timetable for future intelligence.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Chapters 1–3: Boundaries of life and intelligence

- [M1,M3] **Half a Brain** (ch. 1): everyday devices show how capacities and habits form through people using imperfect interfaces. A technology can change its users while users change the technology. Evaluate the combined activity and the particular interface that mediates it, rather than declaring an isolated device autonomous on the strength of a demonstration.
- [M1] **The Meaning of “Life”; Autopoiesis; Homeostasis; Metabolism** (ch. 2): Lee compares candidate characteristics across biological and digital entities. Maintaining a voltage or temperature illustrates homeostasis, but the analogy does not settle whether software is alive. State the definition and where a proposed comparison stops carrying explanatory weight.
- [M1,M3] **Are Computers Useless?** (ch. 3): framing computers as answer machines can obscure the ways they reshape inquiry and human capability. Evaluate the questions and practices made possible by a partnership, not only whether a program independently duplicates a person. Historical observations about intelligence measurements remain examples from the book's period.

### Chapters 4–7: Meaning, feedback, and explanation

- [M1,M3] **Say What You Mean** (ch. 4): fluent outputs and unexpected generative results complicate the idea that a program simply expresses its author's intention. Distinguish the program's mechanics, the user's interpretation, and the larger practice in which the output matters. An impressive artifact does not locate all its meaning in its generator.
- [M1,M3,M4] **Negative Feedback** (ch. 5): a loop corrects behavior through observations of its effects. Fast enough response can make useful performance insensitive to some component uncertainties; delay can destabilize it. Identify the loop, gain, sensing, and timing before equating feedback with reliable self-correction.
- [M1,M3] **Explaining the Inexplicable** (ch. 6): an explanation serves a recipient and purpose. A local account of which inputs influenced a prediction is different from a theory of a whole mechanism or justification for trusting its use. Request the kind of explanation that bears on the decision at hand.
- [M1,M3] **The Wrong Stuff** (ch. 7): embodied mechanisms and environmental engagement challenge the assumption that all intelligence is interchangeable software. A physical design can exploit dynamics unavailable in an input-output benchmark. State which capabilities survive a change in body or environment.

### Chapters 8–11: Computation, intelligence, and responsibility

- [M1,M2,M5] **Am I Digital?** (ch. 8): finite measurements do not establish that what is measured is fundamentally discrete. Lee challenges digital accounts of nature and cognition; these are philosophical arguments. Do not convert either a successful digital simulation or a continuous physical theory into a proof about the thing itself.
- [M1,M2] **Intelligences** (ch. 9): intelligence comparisons need a task and explanatory model. Computational mechanisms can have unfamiliar capabilities without occupying a single human-like scale. Determinism, randomness, novelty, and intelligence are separate claims requiring separate grounds.
- [M1,M3] **Accountability; Who Is the Artist?** (ch. 10): a generated work depends on code, data, training, selection, reuse, presentation, and institutions. Naming one link as creator can hide other contributions. Allocation of credit and responsibility is a decision about the social organization of production, not an automatic output of the algorithm.
- [M1,M2,M4] **Causes** (ch. 11): causal explanations depend on the model and question. Feedback and distributed dependence resist a single linear account. To intervene, identify a manipulable relationship and its assumptions; neither detailed tracing nor complexity by itself answers who ought to act.

### Chapters 12–14: Interaction, pathologies, and selection

- [M1,M4] **Interaction** (ch. 12): systems affect one another and change the conditions under which they operate. Properties of isolated parts need not characterize the joint behavior. A design review must include pathways of response, not just endpoints exchanging messages.
- [M1,M2,M4] **Zero-Knowledge Proofs; First-Person Interaction** (ch. 12): Lee uses interactive protocols to distinguish what a participant can learn from what a third-party observer can establish. The participant's choices can matter to the evidence available. This supports examining the observation and intervention boundary, not a claim that every interactive machine has a self.
- [M1,M2,M5] **Bisimulation; Resolution of Alternatives** (ch. 12): equal observed traces need not establish the same branching structure or when choices are resolved. Lee connects these formal distinctions to agency while explicitly treating the extension to consciousness as conjecture. No mathematical theory of consciousness is supplied; neither a behavioral match nor this analogy settles that question.
- [M1,M3,M4] **Pathologies** (ch. 13): a technology's persistence or spread need not indicate human benefit. Analyze reinforcing incentives, dependence, and the opportunities for correction. Treat the book's technology cases as historical examples, while applying its questions to present evidence.
- [M1,M3,M4] **Coevolution; Digital Creationism** (ch. 14): Lee opposes the image of humans designing the technological world completely from above. Engineers introduce variations into an ecosystem that selects and transforms them. This limits claims of total control while preserving choices about interfaces, institutions, and selection conditions. It does not excuse designers or make harmful outcomes inevitable.

## Worked Example

[M1,M3] **Reconstructed from ch. 10: the generated portrait.** Lee traces a work auctioned under the OBVIOUS collective's presentation to code developed and shared by Robbie Barrat, within a wider chain of algorithms and image material. The model “AI made the painting” leaves out code reuse and human selection. The model “one human authored everything” also discards causal contributors. Map the chain, then distinguish causal production, artistic credit, and responsibility for presentation. The point survives without treating the auction price as a measure of machine intelligence.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- [M1,M3] If someone says technology is inevitable, identify the design or institutional choices the statement conceals.
- [M3,M4] If a feedback system underperforms, inspect delays and what it actually observes before adding more corrective force.
- [M1,M3] If accountability is attributed to an algorithm, trace the people and organizations able to change its inputs, objectives, release, and operation. This is the library's design application of Lee's analysis.
- [M1,M5] If asked when machines will become human-like, replace the timetable with present capabilities, assumptions, and tractable design questions.

## Key Takeaways

1. [M1,M4] Analyze the changing partnership at a declared boundary.
2. [M3] Limited control still leaves consequential choices.
3. [M1,M5] Evolutionary and computational analogies require explicit stopping points.
