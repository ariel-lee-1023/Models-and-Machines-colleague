# Computation and Human Experience — Philip E. Agre
**Format**: md | **Sections**: 14 | **Depth**: study | **Type**: technical

## Mental Model (read first)

[M1,M3,M4] Agre develops **critical technical practice**: critique becomes part of building computational systems rather than a verdict delivered from outside engineering. Recurrent technical difficulties can disclose inherited assumptions about human activity. The alternative centers ongoing involvement in a structured world. M-tags connect these arguments to the user's anchor; Agre does not assert Lee's five theses.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Chapters 1–3: What a working program establishes

- [M1,M3] **Two senses of “working”** (ch. 1): a system may conform to a formal specification while the interpretation attached to it misdescribes human activity. Terms such as planning or understanding do explanatory work beyond naming a procedure. Separate a demonstrated computational result from the substantive claim supposedly demonstrated by it.
- [M1,M3] **Metaphor in Practice; Centers and Margins** (ch. 2): technical vocabulary makes some phenomena central and renders others awkward or peripheral. Inspect what a successful formalism repeatedly treats as an exception. A critique becomes useful when it helps reformulate a technical problem and exposes a different implementable possibility.
- [M1,M4] **Mentalism and Interactionism; Machinery and Dynamics** (ch. 3): mentalism explains action primarily through internal structures; interactionism examines ongoing relations between an agent and its environment. These are methodological orientations, not a simple instruction to eliminate internal machinery. Study how behavior arises over time and which environmental regularities support it.

### Chapters 4–7: Abstraction and maintained dependencies

- [M1,M3] **Architectural and Generative Reasoning** (ch. 4): specifying the organization that could produce behavior is different from accounting for the unfolding production of a particular behavior. A computational architecture is not by itself an adequate explanation of human experience. Inspect the abstraction–implementation relation and the interpretive use of apparently ordinary terms such as variable.
- [M1,M2,M3] **The Digital Abstraction; Embodied Computation** (ch. 5): logic depends on a realized organization with temporal and causal relations. A formal account can suppress timing that matters to the activity it purports to explain. The library's M2 connection is methodological: specify which abstraction has a formal property, rather than attaching it without qualification to an embodied agent.
- [M1,M3,M4] **Dependency Maintenance** (ch. 6): routines can exploit the persistence of conclusions across a changing situation. Track which conditions support which conclusions, then revise affected results when their supports change. This makes ongoing reconsideration affordable where circumstances have structure; it is not a promise to cope efficiently with arbitrarily chaotic changes.
- [M1,M3] **Life Rule System** (ch. 7): Agre's rule language operationalizes dependency maintenance through a maintained pattern of consequences. It is the Life system discussed here, not Conway's Game of Life. Preserve the distinction between an implementation technique and the claim that people literally execute such rules.

### Chapters 8–10: Planning, improvisation, and running arguments

- [M1,M3] **Planning and Execution** (ch. 8): repeatedly alternating a costly planner and executor exposes tensions in the view that plans organize activity by specifying it in advance. An action sequence can look planned retrospectively while having been produced through continual engagement. This is not an assertion that people never plan.
- [M1,M3,M4] **Running Arguments** (ch. 9): continuously reconsider what to do using a structured argument whose dependencies are maintained. Activity can remain coherent as local conditions change. Its efficiency depends on persistence and routines in the world; merely relabeling fast replanning “improvisation” does not remove its computational cost.
- [M1,M3] **Experiments with Running Arguments; Patterns of Transfer** (ch. 10): the RA experiments examine how a chosen organization of goals and arguments affects behavior across circumstances. Examine both successful transfer and its restrictions. A toy environment supports a specific mechanistic demonstration, not a general theory of human improvisation.

### Chapters 11–14: Representation in activity

- [M1,M4] **Representation and Indexicality** (ch. 11): a representation may be intelligible only relative to the agent's current relation to the world. A context-free inventory of named objects is not the only way to support action. Ask what is identified, by whom, during which activity, and through what continuing access.
- [M1,M3,M4] **Deictic Representation; Entities and Aspects** (ch. 12): objects are selected through activity-relative roles and causally maintained relationships, such as an object presently being acted upon. These roles can reduce bookkeeping and connect perception to action. They depend on reliable selection and attention; an indexical expression is not magic access to its referent.
- [M1,M3,M4] **Pengi; Seriality and Focus** (ch. 13): a video-game agent coordinates situated perceptions and actions without storing a complete plan for each routine. The environment and designed circuitry provide much of the organization. Attention and focus remain constraints; the program is a constructive argument whose limits matter as much as its success.
- [M1,M3] **Discourse and Practice** (ch. 14): changes in technical language and changes in machinery should discipline one another. A philosophical alternative earns engineering value by changing what can be built, tested, or explained. Agre's critique remains continuous with existing technique, rather than pretending a complete clean start is possible.

## Worked Example

[M1,M3,M4] **Reconstructed from ch. 13: Pengi's two-ice-cube trick.** A bee is not aligned with an available cube. The agent kicks one cube until another stops it, creating an alignment, then moves behind it and kicks again. An observer can describe this as a plan. Agre instead explains it through circuitry that selects a sensible move in each successive situation and activity-relative representations of relevant objects. The game mechanics carry part of the sequence's organization. To test transfer, change those mechanics or interfere with the relevant visual relation. Success on the original board cannot establish a general capacity for planning or human understanding.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- [M1,M3] When a demo “works,” report both the formal achievement and the interpretation still needing evidence.
- [M1,M4] When a representation grows unwieldy, inspect activity-relative roles and stable environmental relationships before expanding a world inventory.
- [M3,M4] When a routine is efficient, identify what remains stable and who maintains that stability. The second question is a cross-source design application.
- [M1,M3] When critique remains purely verbal, specify a changed representation, mechanism, or test that could reveal its practical consequence.

## Key Takeaways

1. [M1] Technical terms can smuggle substantive theories into a specification.
2. [M3,M4] Coherent activity can be organized through sustained interaction.
3. [M1,M3] Build and critique in the same cycle of inquiry.
