# The Sciences of the Artificial — Herbert A. Simon
**Format**: md | **Sections**: 8 | **Depth**: study | **Type**: text
**Edition**: 2019 reissue of the third edition (1996), with an introduction by John E. Laird. The source claims below concern Simon's eight chapters; Laird's introduction is not silently attributed to Simon.

## Mental Model (read first)

[M1,M3,M4] Simon studies systems adapted to goals and environments, including artifacts, organizations, and cognition. Their behavior can sometimes be understood at an interface without reproducing every internal detail. Search, bounded rationality, and decomposable structure explain how limited agents can design and act. Treat the extension of this framework to human understanding as a substantive hypothesis, not a consensus shared by the other sources.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Chapter 1: Understanding the natural and artificial worlds

- [M1,M3] **The Artifact as Interface**: behavior depends on the relationship between an inner organization and an outer environment under a purpose. A clock unsuitable for a ship might serve on a mantel. When an artifact fails, inspect the match rather than diagnosing the mechanism in isolation.
- [M1,M3] **Functional Explanation; Understanding by Simulating**: a simulation can support understanding by isolating relevant relationships. Behavioral resemblance and identity of internal mechanism are different claims. Specify which features need correspondence for the inquiry; a simulation of one relation does not reproduce the whole thing.
- [M1,M3] **Physical Symbol Systems**: symbols can designate objects, relations, actions, and processes, while physically implemented processes manipulate those structures. Simon's account connects cognition to computation through this architecture. Preserve it as a theoretical commitment that Agre, Winograd and Flores, Smith, and Weizenbaum interrogate from different directions.

### Chapter 2: Economic rationality

- [M1,M3] **Bounded Rationality; Procedural Rationality**: limited knowledge and computational capacity influence what choices can be made. A procedure matters alongside the abstract goal. Evaluate the information and effort available to the decision maker rather than comparing only with an unconstrained optimum.
- [M1,M3] **Satisficing and Aspiration Levels**: search can stop when an alternative meets an acceptable threshold, with aspirations adjusted through experience. This differs from maximizing a fully specified common utility. Ask what counts as acceptable, how alternatives are searched, and when the threshold should change.
- [M1,M3,M4] **Markets and Organizations; Evolutionary Models**: different institutions structure information, incentives, and adaptation. Local success does not guarantee a global optimum. An evolutionary explanation of persistence should not be mistaken for a justification of the resulting arrangement.

### Chapters 3–4: Thinking, memory, and environment

- [M1,M4] **The Ant on the Beach** (ch. 3): complex observed paths can arise from relatively simple adaptive behavior in a complex environment. Simon proposes investigating an analogous hypothesis for human behavior. It is an empirical explanatory proposal, not proof that the whole person has been reduced to a simple machine.
- [M1,M3] **Problem Spaces and Heuristic Search** (ch. 3): representations and operators organize the alternatives available to a problem solver. A difficult search may become tractable after reformulation. Keep the model's state space distinct from the full situation it is meant to represent.
- [M1,M3,M4] **Memory as Environment for Thought; Learning** (ch. 4): stored structures and learned organization affect what a thinker can notice and do. Recognizing and retrieving useful patterns can substitute for fresh search. Do not assume expertise is captured by raw storage capacity or transfer a learned structure without its domain conditions.

### Chapter 5: The science of design

- [M1,M3] **Creating the Artificial**: design concerns courses of action that move an existing situation toward a preferred one. The preference and its owner are part of the problem. Optimizing a given formulation is only part of design when the formulation itself needs development.
- [M1,M3] **Search and Representation**: the representation of a design problem helps determine which solutions are visible and how expensive search becomes. Compare alternative framings before declaring the space exhausted. A formally elegant representation is not necessarily the most useful one for a designer.

### Chapter 6: Social planning

- [M1,M3,M4] **Designing the Evolving Artifact**: social design involves multiple participants and a setting that changes as interventions unfold. Consequences, goals, and knowledge develop together. The library's application is to make objectives revisable and inspect distributional consequences rather than treating society as a passive plant with an uncontested setpoint.

### Chapters 7–8: Complexity and hierarchy

- [M1,M2,M4] **Alternative Views of Complexity** (ch. 7): simple rules, adaptive processes, or nonlinear dynamics can generate difficult-to-predict behavior. Keep complexity of a description, organization, and observed trajectory distinct. Sensitive behavior does not by itself establish model nondeterminism.
- [M1,M3,M4] **Hierarchy; Stable Intermediate Forms** (ch. 8): assemblies that remain intact support the construction of complex systems under interruption. Hierarchy here concerns organization, not necessarily political authority. Examine which intermediate structures can persist and which must be rebuilt.
- [M1,M3,M4] **Near Decomposability** (ch. 8): interactions within subsystems can dominate short-run behavior while weaker between-subsystem interactions shape longer-run aggregates. This permits useful simplification under suitable coupling and timescale conditions. It does not say interfaces can be ignored or that all systems are modular in the required way.

## Worked Example

[M1,M3,M4] **Reconstructed from ch. 8: Hora and Tempus.** Both watchmakers construct complex watches and receive interruptions. Tempus's unfinished assembly falls apart when set down. Hora uses stable subassemblies, so interruption destroys only a small amount of current work. The difference concerns organization, not greater diligence or individual intelligence. In an engineering project, identify which intermediate products remain valid through interruptions or changes. The analogy supports designing stable interfaces and reusable partial results only where actual dependencies permit them.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- [M1,M3] If an artifact fails, inspect its purpose and environment as well as internal construction.
- [M3] If optimization is infeasible, specify a defensible aspiration level and search procedure rather than claiming an unattainable optimum.
- [M1,M4] If behavior looks complex, examine environmental structure before attributing equal complexity to the controller.
- [M3,M4] If proposing decomposition, test coupling and timescales; do not infer independence from a diagram's boxes.
- [M1,M3] If applying this framework to persons, retain competing accounts of background, obligation, and judgment rather than presenting the model as exhaustive.

## Key Takeaways

1. [M1,M3] Purpose, inner organization, and environment jointly define an artifact's success.
2. [M3] Limited agents design through selective search and representation.
3. [M4] Useful decomposition requires a claim about actual interactions.
