# Human–Machine Reconfigurations: Plans and Situated Actions — Lucy Suchman
**Format**: md | **Sections**: 15 | **Depth**: study | **Type**: text
**Edition**: Second edition; copyright 2007, supplied front matter also says first print publication 2006. Locators follow its numbered chapters; the opening Introduction is additional.

## Mental Model (read first)

[M1,M3,M4] Suchman studies how intelligible action is accomplished in particular situations. Plans, instructions, and categories are resources within that activity, not complete accounts of what generates it. The second edition extends the analysis to the production of human and machine agencies, boundaries, and unequal responsibilities. Empirical interaction analysis should not be replaced by either a blanket rejection of machines or a story that human and machine participation is already symmetrical.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Introduction and chapters 1–4: Reopening the problem

- [M1,M3] **Readings and Responses** (ch. 1): “situated action” is not a doctrine that planning is useless or that action has no organization. The question is what plans do in actual practice. Test the interpretation against observed sequences instead of turning the phrase into a new abstract architecture of everything.
- [M1,M3] **Interactive Artifacts** (ch. 4): reactive behavior, linguistic presentation, and opaque internal operations invite users to interpret a machine as an interlocutor. A device designed for a purpose can also be perceived as having purposes. Separate those attributions from what the device can observe and respond to.
- [M1] **Original Preface and Introduction** (chs. 2–3): the earlier study treats human–machine communication as an empirical problem. Its historical setting supports analysis of a particular help system, not an unqualified limit on every possible future machine.

### Chapters 5–7: Plans and communicative resources

- [M1,M3] **Plans** (ch. 5): plans formulate intended courses prospectively and help make actions accountable retrospectively. Their intelligibility depends on circumstances they do not fully enumerate. A plan may orient conduct without specifying every next move. Observe the work of interpreting it instead of classifying all deviations as user error.
- [M1,M4] **Situated Actions** (ch. 6): the sense of an action depends on its position in unfolding activity and on local material and social resources. This does not mean that only the immediately visible present matters. Participants mobilize histories, expectations, and institutions in situ.
- [M1,M3,M4] **Communicative Resources** (ch. 7): people establish and revise intelligibility through sequences, attention, reference, and repair. An utterance is not exhausted by its propositional content. Inspect what each participant can perceive, infer, contest, and repair at that moment.

### Chapters 8–10: Studying asymmetry

- [M1,M3] **Case and Methods** (ch. 8): close analysis of recorded interaction compares users' activity with the machine's access to it. Preserve sequence and concurrent events. An aggregate success metric can erase the repair work that made completion possible; anecdotes detached from sequence can misidentify the source of trouble.
- [M1,M4] **Human–Machine Communication** (ch. 9): the copier help system interprets a restricted set of signals through a prespecified plan. Users draw on much richer circumstances and try to make instructions relevant. A machine's apparent comprehension can therefore depend on the user's interpretive labor.
- [M1,M3] **Conclusion to the First Edition** (ch. 10): improve the design by understanding these limits of access and the resources available to users. The work does not warrant a universal prediction about whether machines could ever interact. It does warrant examining the actual reciprocal resources of a proposed interface.

### Chapters 11–15: Agencies and reconfiguration

- [M1,M3] **Plans, Scripts, and Other Ordering Devices** (ch. 11): inscriptions and procedures help order activity, yet their effects depend on enactment. Ask how a script configures expected users and which adaptations are required to make it work. Written procedure and lived practice are connected without being identical.
- [M1,M3,M4] **Agencies at the Interface** (ch. 12): agency is produced through relations among bodies, artifacts, practices, and institutions. An interface also organizes distributions of capability and responsibility. Analyze what particular arrangements enable without making “distributed” a synonym for equal power or absent accountability.
- [M1,M3] **Figuring the Human in AI and Robotics** (ch. 13): constructions of the human guide what engineers build and what observers recognize as intelligent or social. Examine the assumed person, body, role, and relationship rather than treating “human-like” as a self-explanatory design objective.
- [M1,M3,M4] **Demystifications and Reenchantments of the Humanlike Machine; Reconfigurations** (chs. 14–15): demonstrating internal mechanisms alone does not settle the social effects of personification. Trace the material work and relations that produce the performance, then ask what different arrangements would redistribute agency and responsibility. Reconfiguration is a practical and political inquiry, not merely a vocabulary change.

## Worked Example

[M1,M3,M4] **Reconstructed from chs. 8–9: copier assistance.** Users try to accomplish a copying task while the help system selects instructions from its model of the procedure. Their pauses, interpretation of the physical arrangement, and efforts to repair a misunderstanding may be meaningful to another person yet invisible to the system. The same machine-observed action can therefore correspond to different situations. Analyze the aligned user and machine sequences before revising the instructions. Design implications include making the machine's current interpretation visible and providing recovery resources; these are applications of the case, not claims that Suchman tested a modern interface feature.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- [M1,M3] If users depart from a script, investigate what problem the departure solves before imposing stronger compliance.
- [M1,M4] If an interface appears conversational, inspect mutual detection and repair rather than surface fluency alone.
- [M3,M4] If completion requires invisible assistance, include that work in the assessment and locate who provides it.
- [M1,M3] If agency is called distributed, still identify asymmetric authority, access, and practical responsibility.

## Key Takeaways

1. [M1] A plan is a resource for action, not its exhaustive generating description.
2. [M4] Joint success can conceal unequal access and repair work.
3. [M3] Redesign arrangements as well as interface representations.
