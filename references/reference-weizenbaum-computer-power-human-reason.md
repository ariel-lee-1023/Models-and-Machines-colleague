## Mental Model (read first)

[M1,M3] Weizenbaum asks what is lost when a human concern is recast as a computational problem. A model necessarily selects what matters, and that selection already involves purpose and judgment. Demonstrating a technique's feasibility does not decide whether its use is appropriate, who has authority to choose, or what obligations a person should retain.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Introduction: ELIZA and interpretive investment

- [M1,M3] **ELIZA and DOCTOR** (Introduction, pp. 2–7): a language-processing program combined with a script can sustain the appearance of a therapeutic conversation. Users' responses and proposed professional applications surprised its author. The lesson is about how people interpret a performance and transfer authority to it; it is not evidence that the program understood their lives.
- [M1,M3] **Technique and Human Encounter** (Introduction): Weizenbaum objects to treating a parody of one therapeutic technique as a substitute for the human relationship. Preserve this as his ethical and interpretive criticism, not a clinical effectiveness claim about present-day systems.

### Chapters 1–3: Tools, formal power, and machines

- [M1,M3] **On Tools** (ch. 1): tools transform practices and how people understand themselves, as well as increasing instrumental power. Examine the activity reorganized by a tool and the dependence it creates. The existence of an available instrument does not make its associated way of framing a problem compulsory.
- [M1,M2,M5] **Universality and Effective Procedure** (ch. 2, including pp. 65–66): universal computation concerns effectively specified procedures. Some general questions, such as deciding halting for arbitrary program–input pairs, are not algorithmically decidable. This is a formal boundary, not a proof that people can solve all such questions or that a specific task cannot be automated.
- [M1,M3] **How Computers Work** (ch. 3): symbolic instructions acquire operational effects through an organized physical machine. A program's intelligible expression and the machine's execution are different objects of inquiry. Knowing the mechanics does not settle the meaning or legitimacy of the larger application.

### Chapters 4–5: Abstract machines and purposeful models

- [M1,M2,M3] **Abstract and Physically Embodied Machines** (ch. 4, pp. 111–112): imagined machines can have rules that physical realizations cannot satisfy. A simulation can explore an impossible machine without realizing that machine's physical behavior. The software game has its own rules while the host still obeys physical constraints.
- [M1,M3] **Science and the Compulsive Programmer** (ch. 4): technical mastery can become detached from an external purpose and from human experience. Use this as a critique of practice organized around endless control of the machine, not as a diagnosis of a programmer or a validated psychiatric taxonomy.
- [M1,M3] **Theories and Models** (ch. 5, pp. 132–153): reproducing an observed input-output relation, reproducing an internal mechanism, and explaining a machine at a general computational level are different achievements. Multiple mechanisms can fit observations. Select the explanatory level needed for the question and identify what observations could distinguish alternatives.
- [M1,M3] **Essential Features and Purpose** (ch. 5): the choice of what a model excludes is governed by intended use. Neglecting air resistance can be serviceable for one falling-object question and fatal to an account of a parachute. Technical testing can expose omissions, but judging what counts as essential remains an interpretive and sometimes political act.

### Chapters 6–8: Psychological and linguistic claims

- [M1,M3] **Computer Models in Psychology** (ch. 6): constructing a program that exhibits an effect does not establish that human cognition uses that mechanism. Ask what the model explains, what independent observations support it, and whether a claim changes meaning as it moves between technical and ordinary vocabularies.
- [M1,M3] **The Computer and Natural Language; Artificial Intelligence** (chs. 7–8): linguistic performance and problem-solving demonstrations occur within histories, bodies, and meanings that formal descriptions select only partially. Keep Weizenbaum's substantive and ethical objections distinct from transient limitations of the systems available to him.

### Chapters 9–10: Comprehensibility and responsibility

- [M1,M3] **Incomprehensible Programs** (ch. 9): growth and patching can make a system difficult for its makers to understand while institutions become dependent on it. Complexity does not confer independent legitimacy. Ask whether anyone can evaluate the relevant behavior and take responsibility for continued use.
- [M1,M3] **DENDRAL and MACSYMA** (pp. 229–230): Weizenbaum recognizes technically important achievements in well-structured domains. DENDRAL's candidate molecular structures can be checked against an independent theory relating structure to spectra; MACSYMA manipulates mathematical expressions. His critique is not that every computational application is worthless.
- [M1,M3] **Against the Imperialism of Instrumental Reason** (ch. 10, especially pp. 260, 265): tracing a calculation does not answer the question of a responsible choice. Feasibility does not entail obligation to implement. Research priorities and uses of talent are choices that should be open to ethical deliberation, rather than presented as irresistible technical destiny.
- [M1,M3] **Disagreement with Simon** (p. 260): Weizenbaum resists extending a simple adaptive-system description to the whole person because it can erase choice and obligation. Retain the disagreement about explanatory scope; do not manufacture a shared theory of judgment.

## Worked Example

[M1,M3] **Reconstructed from ch. 5: explorers find a computer.** The explorers can observe input and output but cannot inspect the machine. They might build a behaviorally equivalent device with a different internal mechanism, or discover only a general computational explanation. Each is a meaningful achievement, but neither alone establishes identity of mechanism. The privileged observer's knowledge in the thought experiment makes this difference visible. In a design review, the analogous mistake is to treat matching a benchmark as proof that a system has the same capacities, understanding, or dependencies as the thing benchmarked.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- [M1,M3] If a model claims to capture a person, ask which concerns its variables exclude and who decided they were inessential.
- [M3] If implementation is justified by “it can be done,” request a reason for doing it and identify the person or institution making that choice.
- [M1,M3] If an impressive result has a bounded domain with independent checks, acknowledge that achievement without expanding its scope.
- [M3] If dependence makes replacement difficult, treat that as a governance problem to address, not evidence that the system's authority is deserved.

## Key Takeaways

1. [M1] Behavioral resemblance does not establish an identical mechanism or human significance.
2. [M3] Model selection already contains judgment about purpose.
3. [M3] Technical power leaves the responsibility to choose intact.
